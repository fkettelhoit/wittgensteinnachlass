# Ts-306

### <span class="fac">1\[1\]</span>

What does it mean, then, when I say: The expression of belief is the description of a mental process? That is, if we do not mean by this that it is the indirect description and that there is also a more direct one.

– – – – –

### <span class="fac">1\[2\],2\[1\]</span>

We said that the expression of belief was the description of a mental process, and could no more be put in place of this mental process than the description of toothache could be put in place of the toothache. Let us imagine that what is at issue is the description of a painted picture. We can then say, for instance, that this description could not be put in place of the picture in the sense that it does not serve the same purposes as the picture. The picture, say, is pleasing to the eye, but the description is not, etc. But suppose that the picture is a representation of Napoleon’s coronation. The description of the picture is then a description of this representation and can at any rate serve _one_ purpose that the picture also serves; for the picture and the description of the picture are now representations of Napoleon’s coronation. So although the description is not what is described, it can sometimes serve the same purpose. And the description of a linguistic expression can _only ever_ be used as an expression in place of the expression described. Now what about the description of the process of believing when it reads: “I believe that p will be the case.”? Since, as I am assuming, I can read off the expression of belief directly from the process of believing, or rather derive the expression from that process solely by means of rules of language, that process _could_ also serve me as language; for I have laid down nothing at all as to what I want to call language, except that it must be possible to translate it into our language according to fixed rules.

– – – – –

### <span class="fac">2\[2\]</span>

At first it seemed to us that there could be two descriptions of the process of believing. The direct one told us what went on in our mind, in our brain or elsewhere when we believed. But as we imagined this description, we could not read off from it what was believed. We could not, so to speak, read off the _intention_ of these processes from the processes themselves. But then what was described did not meet the condition that we must be able to tell from the process of believing what is believed. So we could not derive the expression of belief from observing the process of believing unless we were given rules by means of which we could translate from our observation of the process of believing, and hence also from its _description_, into the _expression_ of belief. But then the process of believing as well as its description meets the conditions under which we call something an expression of belief.
