## Generality of a demonstration.

### <span class="fac">[1\[2\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-217/1.webp)</span>

It is as if there were a general _conception_ of the sign (say, of a triangle in a geometrical construction, etc.).

### <span class="fac">[1\[3\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-217/1.webp)</span>

Different rules then apply to the use of the general triangle _than to that of the particular one_. One says: “the size of this triangle does not matter here”.)

### <span class="fac">[1\[4\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-217/1.webp),[2\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-217/2.webp)</span>

Generality of Euclidean proofs. One says that the demonstration is carried out on _one_ triangle, but the proof holds for all triangles – or for any triangle whatsoever. First of all, it is strange that what holds for one triangle should therefore hold for all the others. Surely a doctor could not examine _one_ person and then infer that what he finds in that person must also be true of all the others. And if I now measure and add up the angles in a triangle, I really cannot infer that the sum of the angles will be just as large in every other triangle. It is clear, after all, that the Euclidean proof cannot state anything about a totality of triangles. A proof cannot go beyond itself. But then the construction in the proof is not an experiment, and if it were, the result could not prove anything for other cases. So it is not at all necessary actually to carry out the construction with paper and pencil; rather, the description of the construction must suffice for one to see everything essential from it. (The description of an experiment does not suffice for one to obtain the result of the experiment from it; the experiment must actually be carried out.) The construction in the Euclidean proof is exactly analogous to the proof that 2 + 2 = 4 by means of the Russian calculating machine.

### <span class="fac">[2\[2\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-217/2.webp)</span>

The figure is a sign, not what is designated or an inexact picture of what is designated.

### <span class="fac">[2\[3\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-217/2.webp)</span>

When we carry out a geometrical proof with compasses and straightedge, we use a symbolism with continuous symbols.

### <span class="fac">[2\[4\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-217/2.webp)</span>

If someone were to object to a Euclidean demonstration with straightedge and compasses, “yes, I can see that it holds in this case, but the question is whether it holds in all other cases”, we should have to answer him: “but it does not hold in _this_ case at all”. – And it would be, as already said, just as if someone were to say of the demonstration that p⊃q ∙ p․⊃․q is tautological, “yes, it certainly holds for the letters p and q, but does it hold generally?”

### <span class="fac">[2\[5\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-217/2.webp),[3\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-217/3.webp)</span>

One might think that the general validity of the figure can be justified by sentences like: Every such triangle must have equal sides, because they are the radii of a circle, and therefore these angles must be equal in each one, etc., etc. But that really is _no_ justification. For what do words like “_every_”, etc., mean here? It only looks as though we are dealing with logical inferences here. (Then there follows, again and again, the thought – which, admittedly, I have never regarded as a solution but always as a mere appearance – that the proof is not about a central angle, a circle, etc., at all, but about circularity, the concept of a central angle, etc. Admittedly there is some truth even in this appearance.)

### <span class="fac">[3\[2\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-217/3.webp)</span>

The generality of variables in logic is the generality of demonstration. It consists in this: the fact that p⊃q․ & ․⊃․q is a tautology is _demonstrated with general validity_ using an arbitrarily chosen _particular_ case. I.e., from the demonstration in the particular case I actually see (however it was meant) everything I need in logic. I.e., the demonstration acquires its generality not by being meant that way but by actually demonstrating generally (i.e. with general validity). I.e., the generality here consists in the generality of the application. And this is there, so to speak, whether one wants it or not, simply by virtue of the internal relation of the individual case to the paradigm. – One could then say that a demonstration demonstrates as generally as it can be applied. I.e., it demonstrates generally by virtue of the space in which it is.

### <span class="fac">[3\[3\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-217/3.webp)</span>

There is nothing general in the demonstration; it is entirely particular. But the possibility of applying it contains the generality.

### <span class="fac">[3\[4\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-217/3.webp),[4\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-217/4.webp)</span>

The possibility of application radiates through the space and strikes the body that one brings into this space. One could call the rays of light general, because they illuminate any body whatsoever that gets in their way. But it would be absurd to call the source of light general.

### <span class="fac">[4\[2\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-217/4.webp)</span>

A demonstration demonstrates everything that it demonstrates. Its scope does not depend on how it is _meant_, but _only_ on the demonstration itself. Just as a searchlight sends its light as far as it sends it, however far we mean to send it. That is the difference between a demonstration and a sentence. For in the demonstration nothing is said; rather, something is shown. And so the scope of its application depends on it and its space, but not on us. For one might say: the demonstration is not general at all, but entirely particular. But it does demonstrate something, and that holds as generally as it holds. (The good thing is precisely that wherever else allusions and hints may count, in the demonstration only what is there counts. In this respect it is like an experiment.) Euclid, for example, gives instructions for bisecting a line segment by demonstrating the method (_on an example_). Now, these instructions hold as far as one can apply them. And if one could not apply them in a given case, it would do them no good to have been meant for that case.

### <span class="fac">[4\[3\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-217/4.webp)</span>

The generality of the demonstration is only the space around this demonstration. Application to a particular case is a new body in this space.

### <span class="fac">[4\[4\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-217/4.webp),[5\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-217/5.webp)</span>

To say “yes, the demonstration of this Euclidean proposition with compasses and straightedge does convince me in this case, but how do I know that it also holds in all other cases” is exactly as if one were to say “yes, now at four o’clock the proposition holds, but how do I know whether it holds at every other time”. Anyone who said that would thereby show that they had quite misunderstood the demonstration, its nature. Perhaps they took it for an experiment, in which case the second objection is indeed (as) valid as the first.
