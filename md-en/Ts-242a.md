### <span class="fac">[153\[2\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-242a/153.webp)</span>

<span class="series-number">253.</span> “The other person cannot have my pain.” – That is nonsense. What are _my_ pains? What counts as the criterion of identity here? Consider what makes it possible to speak of “two exactly alike” in the case of physical objects. For example, to say: “This armchair is not the same one that you saw here yesterday, but it is exactly alike.” To the extent that it makes _sense_ to say that my pain is the same as his, to that extent we can both have the same pain. (It would also be conceivable that two people experience pain at the same – not just homologous – location. I don’t know if this is the case with Siamese twins.) I saw someone in a discussion about this subject strike his chest and say: “But he cannot have _**this**_ pain!” – The answer to this is that by emphatically stressing the word “this,” one does not define a criterion of identity. Rather, the emphasis merely reflects the fact that such a criterion already exists and we only need to be reminded of it.

### <span class="fac">[153\[3\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-242a/153.webp),[3\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-242a/3.webp)</span>

<span class="series-number">217.</span> Replacing the word “equal” with “identical” (for example) is also a typical device in philosophy. It is as if we were talking about shades of meaning, and it is only a matter of using our words to hit the right nuance. And that is all that philosophizing is about, only when it is our task to represent the temptation to say this or that in a psychologically precise way. What we are “trying to say” is, of course, not philosophy, but it is its raw material. So, what a mathematician, for example, is inclined to say about the objectivity and reality of mathematical facts is not a philosophy of mathematics, but something that philosophy would have to _deal with_.

### <span class="fac">[166\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-242a/166.webp)</span>

### <span class="fac">[166\[2\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-242a/166.webp)</span>

### <span class="fac">[166\[3\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-242a/166.webp),[167\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-242a/167.webp)</span>

<span class="series-number">242.</span> If I say of myself that I only know, in my own case, what the word “pain” means, must I not say _that_ of others as well? And how can I generalize from the _one_ case in so irresponsible a way? Well, everyone says it of himself, that he only knows, in his own case, what pain is! ‒ ‒ Suppose everyone had a box, in which was something that we call a “beetle.” No one can look inside another's box, and each says that he knows what a beetle is only by looking at his own. – It could be that each one has a different thing in his box. Yes, one could imagine that the thing in the box changed constantly. – But if the word “beetle” still has a use among these people, what is it? – It is not the designation of a thing. The thing in the box is not part of the language-game; not even as an _item_: for the box could be empty. – No, the thing in the box is used to abbreviate; it is a sample of something. That is to say: if one constructs the grammar of the expression of sensation according to the pattern of ‘object and designation,’ then the object falls away as irrelevant to the consideration.

### <span class="fac">[167\[2\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-242a/167.webp),[168\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-242a/168.webp)</span>

<span class="series-number">243.</span> And what should “I only know about my own case…” be as a sentence? An experiential proposition? No. – A grammatical one? So I think: let everyone say of themselves that they only know what pain is from their own pain. – Not that people actually say this, or are even willing to say it. But if everyone said it – it could be a kind of exclamation. And if it is meaningless as a communication, it is still a picture; and why shouldn't we want to conjure up such a picture in our minds? Instead of the words, imagine a painted allegorical picture. Yes, when we reflect philosophically, we often see just such a picture. Formally, a pictorial representation of our grammar. Not facts; but rather, as it were, illustrated expressions.

### <span class="fac">[169\[3\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-242a/169.webp),[170\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-242a/170.webp)</span>

### <span class="fac">[176\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-242a/176.webp)</span>

Is thinking a kind of speaking? One might say that it is what distinguishes thoughtful speaking from thoughtless speaking. – And it seems to be an accompaniment to speech. A process that might also accompany something else, or run independently. – –Say the line: “The pen is probably blunt. Oh, well, it works.” – once thoughtfully; then thoughtlessly; then just think the thought, but without the words. – –Now, during the course of an activity, I could check the tip of my pen, make a face as if I were saying that it is not particularly good, and then continue writing with a gesture of resignation. I could also, while engaged in some measurements, act in such a way that anyone watching me would say that I had – without words – thought: If two quantities are equal to a third, then they are equal to each other. – But what is going on here is not what must accompany the words if they are not spoken thoughtlessly.

### <span class="fac">[176\[2\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-242a/176.webp),[177\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-242a/177.webp)</span>

<span class="series-number">265.</span> Thinking is not an incorporeal process that lends life and sense to speech, and that one could detach from speech, as the evil spirit detaches the shadow of Schlemihl from the ground. But how – “not a corporeal process”? Are there, then, incorporeal processes, and thinking is not one of them? No; I used the word “incorporeal process” to help me, in my confusion, because I wanted to assign a meaning to the word “thinking” in a primitive way. One could, of course, say “Thinking is an incorporeal process” if one wanted to distinguish the grammar of the word “thinking” from that of the word “eating,” for example. But the difference in meaning seems too small. (It is similar when one says that the number signs are real objects, while the numbers are not.) An inappropriate way of expressing oneself is a sure way to remain confused. It, as it were, locks the way out of it.

### <span class="fac">[189\[4\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-242a/189.webp),[190\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-242a/190.webp)</span>

<span class="series-number">293.</span> “I can very well imagine that someone acts in a certain way and yet sees nothing shameful in the action” – and now follows a description of how one should imagine this. “I can imagine a human society in which it is considered indecent to count, except for amusement.” This means roughly: I could easily imagine this image further.

“It makes sense to talk about an endless row of trees; I can imagine that a row of trees continues endlessly.” That is, if it makes sense to say that the row of trees comes to an end here, it makes sense to say that it does not come to an end here, and thus also, that it comes to an end nowhere. The visual image is roughly that of a row of trees that continues ‘infinitely’. Such an image certainly does not guarantee the sense of the expression, nor does it _explain_ it. “I can imagine that our standards shrink as …,” means: If our standards behaved in that way, we would not hesitate to say … This explains the sense of a particular expression. “I can imagine how the other has pain in his stomach!” (I could add: “I am doing it right now.”) Does this explain what it means for the other to have pain? (I can imagine the other’s pain more easily if I am not feeling entirely well myself.)

### <span class="fac">[190\[2\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-242a/190.webp),[191\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-242a/191.webp),[192\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-242a/192.webp)</span>

<span class="series-number">294.</span> The feeling of the unbridgeable gap between consciousness and brain process: How is it that this does not play a role in the considerations of everyday life? The idea of this difference in kind is associated with a subtle dizziness; it occurs when we perform logical tricks. (It is a sign of _confusion_, not of the _difficulty_, of the subject. The same feeling of dizziness overcomes us in certain theorems of set theory.) When, in our case, does this feeling arise? Well, if, for example, I direct my attention to my consciousness in a certain way and say to myself in amazement: _**this**_ is supposed to be produced by a brain process! – as if I were touching my forehead. But what can it mean to “direct my attention to my consciousness”? It is nothing more remarkable than the fact that such a thing exists! What I called this (because these words are not used in everyday life) was an act of looking. I stared straight ahead – but _not_ at any particular point or object. My eyes were wide open, my eyebrows not furrowed (as they usually are when a specific object interests me). No such interest preceded the looking. My gaze was ‘vacant’; or _similar_ to that of a person who admires the illumination of the sky and absorbs the light. Now, consider that there was nothing paradoxical about the sentence I uttered as a paradox (“_this_ is produced by a brain process!”): I could have uttered it during an experiment conducted to show that the light effect I see is produced by the excitation of a particular part of the brain. – But I did not utter the sentence in the context in which it would have had an everyday and non-paradoxical sense. And my attention was not of the kind that would have been appropriate for the experiment. (My gaze would have been ‘intent’, not ‘vacant’.)

### <span class="fac">[192\[2\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-242a/192.webp)</span>

<span class="series-number">295.</span> Here we have a case of introspection; not unlike the one through which James brought out that the ‘self’ consists mainly of ‘peculiar motions in the head and between the head and throat’. And what James' introspection showed was not the meaning of the word “self” (in so far as this means something similar to “person”, “human being”, “himself”, “myself”), nor an analysis of such an entity, but the state of attention of a philosopher who is mentally rehearsing the word “self” and wants to analyze its meaning. (And much can be learned from this.)

### <span class="fac">[192\[3\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-242a/192.webp)</span>

<span class="series-number">296.</span> “People agree that they see, hear, feel, etc. (although some are blind and some are deaf). They thus testify of themselves that they have _consciousness_.” – But how remarkable! To whom am I really communicating when I say “I have consciousness”? What is the purpose of saying this to myself, and how can the other person understand me? – Well, sentences like “I see,” “I hear,” “I am conscious” really do have their use.
