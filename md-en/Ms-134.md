February 28, 1947

As soon as we want to use a schema here, as the forms of expression of our language suggest, & not merely note what is actually common to the concepts, we create a falsely simplified picture of our conceptual world. It is as if we said, _all_ plants have petals; flowers; fruits; seeds.

### <span class="fac">[1\[4\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-134/1.webp)</span>

### <span class="fac">[2\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-134/2.webp),[3\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-134/3.webp)</span>

March 1, 1947

If one always represents everything through outlines, one cannot represent a gradual transition. If one were accustomed to representing all whole objects in a straight line, it would be difficult to break free from the prejudice that a curved line could be something other than a fragment. We perceive what looks like a fragment as a whole. This is similar to replacing the dots in “a, a<sup>2</sup>, a<sup>4</sup>, a<sup>8</sup>… with a sign that does not seem to express indeterminacy. One sets aside one comparison and suggests another. But why should I be so concerned with introducing one comparison at the expense of another? What do I hope to achieve with it? Well, a comparison has consequences. _One_ is a recurring illusion of the mind, the feeling that we are walking on air, without touching solid ground, & saying things that are either completely meaningless or actually too much for us.

### <span class="fac">[3\[2\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-134/3.webp),[4\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-134/4.webp),[5\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-134/5.webp)</span>

But most of mathematics, for example, takes place without philosophical nonsense or philosophical questions entering into it. And why shouldn't one simply gloss over that little bit of nonsense with silence? Who has ever been harmed by the fact that the preface or introduction to a book on experimental physics mentions the law of causality?

Or what does it matter if Hardy writes that he believes that the sentences of mathematics correspond to an objective reality? – Well, that is not easy to answer. – And above all, a sensible person could also easily navigate past all these little philosophical fogs. Of course, there are then areas where further stretches of land are covered with fog. And now one could object to philosophy that precisely where the fogs are most widespread, in economics, for example, they are not the result of a convoluted process of conceptualization, but are created by certain interests that use the conventional concepts as an instrument. And one can doubt whether modifying the concepts will achieve a far-reaching clarification of people's thinking. I don't know what one can say in reply, except that philosophy is a human activity with a direction, but with as uncertain a success as any other human activity.

### <span class="fac">[5\[2\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-134/5.webp),[6\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-134/6.webp)</span>

March 2, 1947

A smell can be very pleasant. Is the fact that it is pleasant a sensation? Then the sensation of pleasantness would accompany the smell. But how would it relate to it? Of course, the expression of pleasantness is, in its nature, similar to the expression of a sensation, especially pain. But joy has no location; there are joyful thoughts, but not toothache-like ones. But – one might say – whether joy is a sensation or _what_ it is, one must notice it when one has it! – (And why especially when one _has_ it, & not when one _does not have_ it?) Do you also notice the _essence_ of one when you eat _an_ apple, & the _essence_ of zero when you eat none?

### <span class="fac">[6\[2\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-134/6.webp)</span>

When Frege said that a number is not a heap, – what led him to this conclusion, what did he consider in order to find this out?

### <span class="fac">[6\[3\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-134/6.webp),[7\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-134/7.webp)</span>

Is pain always unpleasant? Is it always so, for example, when sexual stimuli are associated with it? Or will you say: “Pain in itself is always unpleasant”? This would mean something like: it is unpleasant, but one gladly endures it for the sake of other sensations. – But can pain be pleasant, like a pleasant smell? It is certainly possible that it is unpleasant for me to encounter a pleasant smell; & pleasant to find an unpleasant one. And here one can say: the smell (in itself) is not unpleasant, but it is unpleasant for me to encounter it here. But is this distinction always possible? Can one always say, for example, that a taste is pleasant or unpleasant _in itself_? Is it the case with pain, as with the bitter taste, which is much more often unpleasant than pleasant, so that one often speaks of bitter pain & sweet joy, but rarely of sweet pain & bitter joy? But here one must again be wary of theorizing. For why should pain & smell have the same internal relationship to pleasure & displeasure?!

### <span class="fac">[7\[2\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-134/7.webp)</span>

### <span class="fac">[11\[4\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-134/11.webp),[12\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-134/12.webp)</span>

“The smell is wonderful!” Is there any doubt that it is the smell that is wonderful? So, is it a property of the smell? – Why not? It is a property that ten is divisible by two, and also, that it is the number of my fingers. But there might be a language in which people simply close their eyes and say “Oh, this smell!” and there is no S.P. sentence that is equivalent to it. That is simply a ‘specific’ reaction.

### <span class="fac">[12\[2\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-134/12.webp),[13\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-134/13.webp)</span>

I know his convictions from his actions, but not my own from my actions. But how does it show that I know my convictions _before_ their manifestation? That is to say: how does it show that I have my convictions before their manifestation? How does it show that I do not derive my knowledge of my convictions from my actions? – Well, I manifest them without inferring them from my behaviour. – But is it then really the same thing as what I infer from his behaviour? – But I do infer his convictions both from their manifestation and from his actions. Now, therefore: is what he says he has, & what I say I have, without our inferring it from any observation, the same as what we infer from the observation of the other’s behaviour & from his conviction’s **manifestation**?

### <span class="fac">[14\[3\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-134/14.webp)</span>

Imagine an observer who, as it were, automatically utters his observations. Yes, he hears himself speak, but he doesn't, as it were, take any note of it. He sees that the enemy is approaching and reports it, describes it, but like a machine. What would that be like? Well, he doesn't act according to his observation. One could say of him that he says what he sees, but he doesn't _believe_ it. It doesn't, as it were, penetrate him.

### <span class="fac">[15\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-134/15.webp),[16\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-134/16.webp)</span>

Conviction (belief) as a state of mind. A state from which actions and words flow. And from the words flow again actions of others. “He is convinced of it” – he is ‘permeated’ by it. What would it be like: to infer this state of my mind from my words? – It would only be possible – one might say – if the words themselves had been uttered automatically. But what do I mean by that? That my words were spoken in an automatic, expressionless tone? Is that essential? No. But if I said “From my words… I see that I am permeated by the fact that…”, then one would say: You are speaking of yourself as of another, so as if there were two people living in you, or as if there were a person and an automaton. “It is as if you were treating your own words like a rash, from which you infer a state of your skin.”

### <span class="fac">[16\[2\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-134/16.webp)</span>

Why do I not infer a state from my own words, from which words and actions spring? Above all, I do not infer my likely actions from my words.

### <span class="fac">[16\[3\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-134/16.webp)</span>

Asked: “Will you act like that?” – I consider _reasons_ and _counter-reasons_.

### <span class="fac">[16\[4\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-134/16.webp)</span>

What could one infer from the words “It is raining, and I don't believe it”? Perhaps, that it is raining – if I am a good observer – and that I will act like someone who doesn't believe it. This presupposes that one interprets the statement in an _abnormal_ way; namely, that the first part is about the weather, but the second is not, but about me.

### <span class="fac">[17\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-134/17.webp)</span>

How would one have to imagine people who play such a language-game? People who repeatedly make communications, statements, assertions, with an addition that describes the state of their conviction independently of that statement. The question, of course, is: how can what brings them to the statement not also convince them of it? He sees the rain – therefore he says “It is raining”; must he not then also believe it for the same reason? How, if he said: “I see that it is raining; & I don't believe it”? (This would also make no sense in the third person.)

### <span class="fac">[17\[2\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-134/17.webp),[18\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-134/18.webp)</span>

But consider: I do sometimes take the other person's word – so I must at least sometimes also take my own word for it that I am of this or that conviction. But if I report my observation in a quasi-automatic way, then this report has nothing to do with my conviction. However, I could trust myself, or my observing self, just as much as another person does. So I could say: “I say ‘it is raining,’ so it probably is.” Or: “The observer in me says ‘it is raining,’ & I am inclined to believe him.” – Isn’t it like that – or similar – when one says that God spoke to him, or through his mouth?

### <span class="fac">[18\[2\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-134/18.webp),[19\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-134/19.webp)</span>

“It will rain; & if you ask me, I will say that it will not rain.” What do I assume when I assume that it is raining & I don't believe it? I assume that it is raining & that, for example, if asked “Is it raining?” I will answer with conviction, no. – So if I assert what I am here assuming, then I assert: it is raining, & if asked, for example, whether it is raining, I will answer with conviction: No. So you are, as it were, two people.

### <span class="fac">[19\[2\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-134/19.webp)</span>

The important insight is that there is a language-game in which I, automatically, make a communication that can be treated by others in exactly the same way as a non-automatic one – except that here there is no question of him ‘lying’ – & that I myself can receive it as the communication of a third person. One could also call the ‘automatic statement, communication, etc.’ an ‘oracle.’ – This, of course, means that the oracle (then) must not use the words “I believe ‥․.”

### <span class="fac">[21\[4\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-134/21.webp),[22\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-134/22.webp),[23\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-134/23.webp)</span>

“The words ‘It is raining’ are written in his mind.” This is meant to say as much as, that is, it can be replaced by, “He believes that it is raining.” “The words ‘It is raining’ are written in my mind” – this is roughly the same as: “I cannot rid myself of the belief that…”, “The idea has taken possession of me that…”. Note that the words “I believe it is raining” & “It is probably raining” can say _the same thing_: _insofar_ as, in certain contexts, it makes no difference which we use. (And free yourself from the idea that one is accompanied by a different mental process than the other!) The two sentences can say the same thing, although the first corresponds to “I believe…”, “He believes…”, etc., and the second does not. The first is simply formed with a different _concept_. That is: in order to say that it might be raining, we do not need the concept of “belief”, although we can use it for that purpose. The concept that a sentence is ‘written in one’s mind’ is now a third concept, which, in its applications, partially overlaps with the others, and partially does not. I mean that, in order to form the statement “It is probably raining”, one does not need the ‘odd’ concept of ‘belief’, although one can use it.

### <span class="fac">[23\[2\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-134/23.webp)</span>

### [VB](/w-vb/#ms-134-28.2) <span class="fac">[28\[2\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-134/28.webp)</span> {#282}

The mathematician can, of course, marvel at the wonders (the crystal) of nature; but can he do so when it has become problematic, what he sees? Is it really possible, as long as a philosophical obscurity obscures what is the wondrous or marvel-inducing thing?
