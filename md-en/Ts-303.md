# Ts-303

### <span class="fac">[1\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/1.webp)</span>

The ordinary way of expressing oneself, “I have toothache”, “N. has toothache”, groups things together grammatically in a way that fits the facts in much the same way as a geometry can fit the facts of physics. That is, another geometry would do as well, but _this_ one allows a particularly simple representation of the facts. (This is not, of course, to say that geometry, or grammar in general, is a representation of the facts; it is only that certain facts lead us to prefer this geometrical, or more generally grammatical, representation.) So it is no wonder that certain facts about our bodies, to which we are at least as accustomed as we are to the solidity of wood, iron or stone, have channelled our grammar in quite definite directions.

### <span class="fac">[1\[2\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/1.webp)</span>

I have the following facts in mind here: that we see with our eyes and hear with our ears; that my hand looks much the same today as it did yesterday; that it has never happened that on waking I saw attached to my arm a hand I recognized as my friend’s, and my own attached to his arm; that a certain experience I call the muscular feeling of bending one of my fingers has never coincided regularly with the bending of a finger of any body other than my own; and so on.

### <span class="fac">[1\[3\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/1.webp),[2\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/2.webp)</span>

It hardly needs saying: we are not concerned with problems about toothache. Those would be problems, say, for dentistry or psychology. And further, the use of the word “toothache” is in no way problematic for us as long as we are using the word in practice. The difficulty arises only when we look at the use of the word from outside. Suddenly, when we speak of toothache, there seems to be a problem; and we feel as though earlier, in everyday life, we had passed heedlessly by a difficulty that now presents itself to us. The problem seems in a way to be a scientific one; it concerns, one would like to say, the _essence_ of pain.

### <span class="fac">[2\[2\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/2.webp)</span>

Philosophy looks at toothache, and there is the problem. It is as if philosophy were looking at the object, say a door, through a refracting medium, and the door now looked bent to us, although we can still go in and out through it as before. This is how the _typical_ question arises: “How is … possible at all?” We ask this only when the facts _astonish_ us, when something about them seems downright incongruous. Different forms of expression in language act like media with different refractive indices and make the object look bent when I represent it half through one and half through the other.

### <span class="fac">[2\[3\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/2.webp)</span>

There may be a problem as to whether a person A has toothache or not. It may be difficult to establish that. In some cases we say: I surmise that he has toothache; in others: I know for certain that he has toothache. And yet one also says: “I can never _know_ whether the other person has toothache; I can only surmise that he does.” What drives us to say this is the need to emphasize a difference between the grammar of the sentence “I have toothache” and the grammar of the sentence: “The other person has toothache”. The way I do this is misleading, for it makes it look as if I wanted to say that in the one case I cannot attain the (logically attainable) ideal of knowledge, whereas what is to be said is: I wish to exclude the word “knowledge” from this context. Used in this way, knowledge is not a limiting value of surmise. And of course this rule about the use of the words knowledge and surmise does not say either that I was mistaken when I said: “I know that he has toothache”, as opposed to the case in which I merely surmised it. Indeed, the new way of speaking will have to give us a means of expressing precisely this contrast.

### <span class="fac">[2\[4\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/2.webp),[3\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/3.webp)</span>

So we say, misleadingly: “I cannot know whether the other person has toothache, but can only surmise it”; but then the question arises: How is it even possible to surmise it if I _cannot_ know it? Is it not in any case a wholly superfluous assumption, then, that the other person has toothache? Should I not simply say: “Only I have toothache, not the other person; he merely behaves as I do when I have toothache”? Someone who says this cannot, of course, help feeling a little ashamed in front of the others. He would then like to say: “It is true, but one cannot say it because of course the others will not accept it.” And one counters him with common sense, which says that the others can after all have toothache even if he does not feel it; and that this is surely easy to imagine, since the others would then simply have what _he_ has when he has toothache. And now it almost seems to him as though everything were clear and there were no difficulty at all.

### <span class="fac">[3\[2\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/3.webp)</span>

But the difficulty is still there. For the model we have in mind when we say, for instance, “He now has what I used to have” or “why should he not be able to have what I have?” is the use of the word “have” in sentences like “he now has the watch I used to have”; “he has bad teeth, and I have bad teeth”; “he has white hair and I have white hair”. In exactly the same sense one can say that my right hand now has something that my left hand then has (say, a disease), that this or that tooth in my mouth has a filling, and also that this or that tooth in my mouth has pains. And if I now say _in this sense_ that the other person has what I had, this transition takes us not from my toothache to his toothache, but (as I should now have to say) from my toothache in my mouth to my toothache in his mouth.

### <span class="fac">[3\[3\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/3.webp),[4\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/4.webp)</span>

“Each person feels only his own pain, not the other person’s”. When we say this, we do not want to sum up our experience so far. If we did, the use of the expression “A feels B’s pain” would first have to be specified more closely; for instance, experiences that were to count as criteria for this would have to be described to us. But what one actually wants to say is that it is logically impossible for A to feel B’s pain. One is thus laying down a rule for the use of language, and what one should really say is: it is senseless to say “A feels B’s pain”. But then it is also senseless to say: “A feels his own pain”. The same holds for the expression: “I know my own pains directly, those of others indirectly”. I can, of course, reserve the words directly and indirectly for my own pains and for those of others respectively, but then the words “I” (or “my”) and “directly” form a linguistic unit; they cannot be separated; they are as it were correlative; and likewise “the other person” and “indirectly”. The rule then says, as it were, that it is fitting to speak of “directly” and so on in the case of one’s own pain.

### <span class="fac">[4\[2\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/4.webp)</span>

This doubling of the distinctions is therefore unnecessary, and this indicates that we can use the second alone in place of the first, that is, speak of direct and indirect pains instead of my pains and the other person’s pains.

### <span class="fac">[4\[3\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/4.webp)</span>

Our saying “I feel my pains directly, but experience the other person’s only indirectly” may count as a sign that we are inclined to give up the possessive way of speaking here. That disguised grammatical sentence is really introducing a new way of speaking. Yet it is this sentence that seems to state the metaphysical exceptional position of the I (as opposed to an empirical exceptional position of a human body), although the I has no such exceptional position in either of the ways of speaking the sentence employs. We can hardly help believing that there is something almost mysterious about the I and the He, and about the relation of my feeling to his; as though there were something here that is hard to grasp, something that resists being grasped by our minds. We seem to have to _overcome_ a resistance, whereas it vanishes when we adjust our language correctly. (A door that is hard to open, namely, one that can be opened only by applying great force, and on the other hand a safe door with a combination lock.)

### <span class="fac">[5\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/5.webp)</span>

(If the location of my pains were someone else’s tooth, I could quite well say: I feel the other person’s pains. But this is not what one wants; rather, one wants to say: Nonsense, I can only ever feel _my_ pains, even if they are in the other person’s tooth. In this way one shows that one would like to distinguish the location and the possessor of the pains. Even though another body is the location (the “bearer”) of the pains, I am still to remain their possessor; it is supposed to be _nonsensical_ to say “I feel someone else’s pains”. But then it is also nonsensical to say: “I can only feel _my_ pains”; for there would be sense in saying this only if it could logically be otherwise (if that “cannot” expressed only an empirical impossibility). So if it is nonsensical to say: “I cannot feel someone else’s pains”, if pain and possessor thus essentially coincide and cannot be separated, then there is no sense at all in speaking of a possessor of the pain. That would make sense only if the pain could change possessors.)

### <span class="fac">[5\[2\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/5.webp)</span>

The sentence “I perceive my toothache directly, the other person’s indirectly” has all the appearance of a statement about an object, and yet it is only establishing a way of expressing oneself. Reality can neither agree nor disagree with it. So it cannot set the I in opposition to the Other either; it cannot give the I an exceptional position at all.

### <span class="fac">[5\[3\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/5.webp)</span>

Do we grant the length of the human cubit an exceptional position by stipulating it as the unit of length? Yes and no.

### <span class="fac">[5\[4\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/5.webp),[6\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/6.webp)</span>

If one says: “I cannot feel the other person’s pain, but only my own”, one could also express the view stated in this as follows: My pain is not distinguished from his by its _location_. On this view I can say: “I have pains in the other person’s hand, in A’s, B’s, C’s etc.” So if I call human bodies A, B, C, the possessor of the pain is not specified by my saying that the pain is in the hand of body A. At the very least, it is supposed to hold of _my_ pain that its location in a particular human body does not characterize it as mine.

### <span class="fac">[6\[2\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/6.webp)</span>

If I ask what characterizes it as such, the first answer is that “I feel it directly”. But what if I felt it indirectly? Is this way of speaking supposed to make no sense? Then I can leave out the word “I” in the preceding sentence and say that what characterizes my pain is that it is felt directly (“it thinks”). Mine, then, is the one that is felt directly, and I know that another person is in pain only indirectly, from his manifestations of it.

### <span class="fac">[6\[3\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/6.webp)</span>

But _this is a stipulation_. For we speak of those manifestations as indications of pain, and this sounds as though we had contrasted indirect knowledge of the other person’s pain with direct knowledge of the _other person’s_ pain. We can speak of knowing directly that someone is in the next room if we see him there through the doorway; indirectly if we merely hear him speaking. What we hear can then, for instance, lead us to open the door and now see him directly in the other room.

### <span class="fac">[6\[4\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/6.webp)</span>

We are now tempted to assume that the grammar of this case is also that of the _preceding_ case. Our way of expressing ourselves makes it seem as if we could not get to perceive the other person’s pain directly, in the sense in which we cannot get into the other person’s room when the door is locked, – whereas we have in fact _stipulated_ that certain phenomena are to be called “_indications of pain_ in the other person” and have not set a perception of the other person’s pain in contrast to the perception of the indications.

### <span class="fac">[6\[5\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/6.webp)</span>

All misunderstandings in this area arise because we are not using “directly” and “indirectly” here as possible arguments of the same function, and so not as opposites at all.

### <span class="fac">[6\[6\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/6.webp),[7\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/7.webp)</span>

Someone who says: “Two people cannot feel the same (identical) pain” can be asked: “What about the case of Siamese twins, for instance? They might feel pain at some point in their double body. Now was that one pain, or were there two pains, and what kind of investigation is it to find out whether it was one or the other?” I assume here that the statements “Each of the two has a different pain” and “they both have the same pain” are meant to be opposed statements. So if one applies, the other must be false, not senseless. There would therefore have to be criteria in experience by which I would say one thing on one occasion and the other on another (a criterion for the first statement might be, for instance, that one says he has a stabbing pain and the other that he feels a painful pressure). But this is not at all the kind of case I wanted to consider. I wanted to make a metaphysical decision; I did not want to distinguish two experiences from one another at all. – Well, then I do not need a way of speaking that sounds like a decision between two opposed statements either. For suppose I said “Each of the two has his own pain, even if the two pains differ neither in intensity nor in location, etc.”; there is no case opposed to this in which the two share a pain, and so in the sentence “A has his pain” the word “his” is superfluous.

### <span class="fac">[7\[2\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/7.webp)</span>

Let us imagine the following case: In a community, one person, A, is particularly skilful at making clay vessels; the others cannot make them but can draw them. Under these circumstances a way of speaking has become established in which by “A’s vessel” one means what we understand by “vessel”; by “B’s, C’s, D’s, etc., vessel” one means drawings of vessels made by B, C, D, etc. I assume there is also a particular expression for the drawing of a vessel that A has made.

### <span class="fac">[7\[3\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/7.webp),[8\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/8.webp)</span>

There is no need to explain how the way of expressing oneself just described can be misleading. But could one call it wrong? Certainly not! One can take it to task for grouping unlike things together – but why should it not do that? Suppose that in the language described above the expressions “A’s tool”, “B’s tool”, etc., mean what we would ordinarily understand by them; then the way of expressing oneself with “A’s vessel”, etc., will be particularly misleading. One will now be able to say in various cases that one is speaking of a vessel of A’s as opposed to a vessel of B’s (even though this contrast is of a quite different kind from that between a tool of A’s and a tool of B’s). But what if I said, for instance: “This vessel of A’s is made of clay”? Can I say here that I have spoken of a vessel of A’s as opposed to a vessel of B’s? No, for the expression “B’s vessel” would make no sense here if substituted for the other.

### <span class="fac">[8\[2\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/8.webp)</span>

This example shows a case of what one might call an asymmetrical application of the possessive, and it is easy to derive from it the various philosophical misunderstandings that have actually arisen in similar cases in our language. In a case in which the subject _I_ is not opposed to any other subject – namely because the grammar of the other parts of the sentence ensures that the word I cannot be replaced with any of its usual opposites without loss of sense – in such a case the use of the word I becomes obsolete.

### <span class="fac">[8\[3\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/8.webp)</span>

Can a machine think? We remind ourselves that thinking is not necessarily a succession or series of images (I mean images of the imagination). Thinking can equally well consist in writing, reading, drawing, calculating. Well, then a machine can think if it can, for instance, write or draw.

### <span class="fac">[8\[4\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/8.webp),[9\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/9.webp)</span>

But perhaps this does not satisfy us, and we say: “But the machine does not know that it is writing. What we mean by ‘thinking’, after all, is the personal experience of writing, reading, etc., and the machine does not have that”. If we say this, then the exact analogue of our first question is: “can a machine have pains?” Well, can a human body have pains? Insofar as one can say the one, one can also say the other. Now one would like to say: “But the body cannot have pains either!” Yet insofar as the body is not the bearer or possessor of the pain, the pain has no possessor. One objects: “But _I_ have pains, not my body, for I could have pains even without a body (in the amputated hand), and I could also change my body.” But why do I call it my body then? as opposed to what? Here I refer back to the preceding investigation.

### <span class="fac">[9\[2\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/9.webp)</span>

The expression “red and green at the same time in the same place” is formed according to various analogies.

### <span class="fac">[9\[3\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/9.webp)</span>

Let us consider first that the sense of the sentence does not hover above it like some ethereal entity but lies in the grammatical stipulations concerning its parts. This explains how a sentence can “sound as though it makes sense”: it has the familiar ring of a sentence, and consists of familiar combinations of words.

### <span class="fac">[9\[4\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/9.webp)</span>

Let us look at the analogies on which the sentence “green and red are here at the same time” is modelled. One of them, for example, is “This paper is green and round”. Consider here the variety in the grammar of subject and predicate. Imagine someone replying to the sentence “This paper is green and round”: “Here there’s green (pointing his finger at the centre of the paper), but it’s not round here! So it isn’t green where it’s round.”

### <span class="fac">[9\[5\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/9.webp),[10\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/10.webp)</span>

Can one give the sentence “This patch is both red and green at the same time” a sense? Certainly! for instance, the sense of the sentence: this patch is partly red and partly green. But if we give the sentence this sense, have we not departed from the norm for giving such sentences sense? That is, have we not given it a disparate sense? Have we not, as it were, cheated the person we told that the sentence could perfectly well have a sense, namely the one just given? What this really means is: have we actually followed the analogies that ought to have guided us in giving it a sense?

### <span class="fac">[10\[2\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/10.webp)</span>

Consider the following case. We explain to someone what a regular quadrilateral inscribed in a circle is; then a triangle and a digon. Now we ask him to draw a regular monogon by analogy, or we say that he _cannot do it_. But what if he marks a point on the circumference of a circle and says that this is the regular monogon? How can we take him to task for not having drawn it correctly by analogy? Well, it has no extension, after all, and the previous polygons did have extension. But where did we get the concept of extension from? From those very polygons, of course, and what we wanted was precisely that he should not draw one of these. It was we ourselves who stipulated, as the series of analogous constructions, one that began with the regular digon, or perhaps even with the regular triangle; and what would we say in reply to someone who said: The regular triangle is not analogous to the regular quadrilateral either, for in the latter a side is opposite a side, while in the former a side is opposite an angle!

### <span class="fac">[10\[3\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/10.webp)</span>

We must become aware of _where_ we get a concept from. We say, for example, that there are six primary colours, and that it is impossible to find a seventh. But what analogy are we to follow here? We do not consider that the concept “primary colour” is given here by listing six colours, i.e. that, for example, the sentence “this sphere has a primary colour” means _the same_ as: “this sphere is either green or blue or red or yellow or white or black”.

### <span class="fac">[10\[4\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/10.webp),[11\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/11.webp)</span>

Compare with this: How did one arrive at the concept “trisection of the angle with straightedge and compasses”? There is metric n–section, and there is a series of divisions with straightedge and compasses. Compare the concept “trisection of the angle with straightedge and compasses” with the concept “cardinal number whose square is 10”.

### <span class="fac">[11\[2\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/11.webp)</span>

Think of a geometry whose only construction is the bisection of a line segment with straightedge and compasses. In this geometry one can divide the line segment into _4_, _8_, etc., equal parts by successive bisection, but in it there is no trisection of the line segment. Investigate this case!

### <span class="fac">[11\[3\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-303/11.webp)</span>

We ask the person who says that there is no surface that is both red and blue at the same time to explain to us what he means by these words. We shall not say that he _cannot_ explain it to us. He can perfectly well explain it, but perhaps he _will_ not explain it. Perhaps he will not _want_ to give this combination of sounds a sense; just as he could also explain the word “regular monogon”, indeed he was quite free to do so, but perhaps he did not want to explain it and therefore stopped at the regular digon. If one asks: But why did he not want to go further? one may reply: because the system seemed complete to him _that way_. But in saying this one may be pointing to a feeling of satisfaction, perhaps to the satisfaction afforded by a particular analogy with other systems. He closes off the system at this point in his explanations of the language, because it suits him here, but then forgets the boundaries he himself has drawn, say by his ostensive definitions; and he looks for formations analogous to those of other systems in the very place where, by drawing his boundaries in the first place, he ruled out just this analogy.
