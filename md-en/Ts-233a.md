### [Z](/w-z/#ts-233a-6.1) <span class="fac">[6\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-233a/6.webp)</span> {#61}

<span class="series-number">225.</span> If I allude to N with a remark, this—given certain circumstances—may be discernible from my gaze, facial expression, etc.

The fact that you understand the expression “to allude to N” can be demonstrated by describing examples of allusion. What will you describe? Primarily, the circumstances. Then, what someone says. Perhaps also his gaze, etc. Then, what the person making the allusion wants to do. And if I also share my feelings, images, etc., with someone while making the remark, these may complete the typical image of the allusion (or _an_ image of this kind). But it does not follow from this that the expression “to allude to N” means: to behave in such a way, to feel this, to imagine this, etc. And here some will say: “Of course not! We have always seen that. And there must be a common thread running through all these phenomena. It is, as it were, interwoven with them, and therefore difficult to find.” – And that is not true either.

But it would also be wrong to say that “alluding” denotes a family of mental and other processes. – For one can ask “Which was your allusion to N?”, “How did you make it clear to the other person that you meant N?”; but not: “How did you intend this utterance as an allusion to N?” “I alluded to him in my speech.” – “With which words?” – “I alluded to him when I spoke of a man who…” “I alluded to him” means approximately: I _wanted_ someone to think of him when hearing these words. But “I wanted” is not a description of a state of mind, and “understanding that N was meant” is not either.

One does, however, ask: “With which remark did you allude to him?”, “With which remark did you mean him?”.

### [Z](/w-z/#ts-233a-7.1) <span class="fac">[7\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-233a/7.webp)</span> {#71}

<span class="series-number">242.</span> I expect an explosion every moment. I am unable to give my full attention to something else; I look at a book, but without reading. When asked why I seem distracted or nervous, I say that I expect an explosion every moment. – What was it like then: did this sentence describe just that behaviour? But how does the process of expecting an explosion differ from the process of expecting a completely different event, e.g. a specific signal? And how does the expectation of _one_ signal differ from the expectation of a slightly different signal? Or was my way of acting merely a side effect of the actual expectation, and was this a mental process? And was this process homogeneous, or structured like a sentence (with _internal_ beginning and end)? – But how does the one in whom it is happening know what event the process is the expectation of? He does not seem to be in doubt about it. It is not as if he were stating a mental or other state and making a guess about its cause. He might say “I do not know if it is only this expectation that is making me so restless today”; but he would not say: “I do not know if this state of mind I am now in is the expectation of an explosion, or of something else.” The statement “I expect an explosion every moment” is a _manifestation_ of the expectation. This verbal reaction is the deflection of the pointer that indicates what is expected.

Let us imagine that someone is performing a task involving comparisons, trials, and choices. He is, for example, constructing an object from certain pieces of material using given tools. Again and again, the problem arises: “Should I take _this_ piece?” – The piece is rejected, another is tried. Pieces are provisionally put together and taken apart; a suitable one is sought, and so on. I now imagine this entire process filmed. The worker also makes sounds, such as “Hm” or “Ha!” – sounds that might be described as expressions of hesitation, sudden discovery, decision, satisfaction, or dissatisfaction. But no words are spoken. These sounds may be recorded in the film. The film is shown to me, and I now invent an internal monologue of the worker, which fits his method of working, the rhythm of his work, his facial expressions, his gestures, and his natural sounds, which corresponds to all of this. I have him say, for example, “No, this piece is too long; perhaps another would fit better.” – – Or “What should I do now?” – “I’ve got it!” – – Or “This is quite good,” etc. If the worker could speak – would it be a distortion of the actual process if he described it accurately and said, for example, “Then I thought: No, that won’t work; I must try it differently,” and so on – even though he did not speak during the work and did not even have these words in mind? I mean, can he not reproduce his wordless thoughts later in words? In such a way that we, who saw the working process, could agree with this reproduction? – Especially if we had observed the man at work not just once, but several times?

### [Z](/w-z/#ts-233a-72.3) <span class="fac">[72\[3\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ts-233a/72.webp)</span> {#723}

<span class="series-number">391</span> “It is as if our concepts are conditioned by a framework of facts.” This meant: if you think of certain facts differently, if you describe them differently from how they are, then you can no longer imagine the application of certain concepts, because the rules of their application have no analogue under the new circumstances. – What I am saying, therefore, comes down to this: a law is given for people, and a lawyer may well be capable of drawing consequences for every case that usually occurs to him; the law therefore obviously has its use, a sense. Nevertheless, its validity presupposes all sorts of things; and if the nature of what it has to judge deviates completely from that of the ordinary person, then, for example, the decision as to whether he committed a deed with evil intention will not merely be difficult, but (simply) impossible.
