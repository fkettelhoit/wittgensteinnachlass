## Foreword to the Dictionary for Elementary Schools

The present dictionary is intended to meet an urgent need in current spelling instruction. It arose from the author’s practice: in order to improve the spelling of his class, the author found it necessary to provide his pupils with dictionaries, so as to enable them to check the spelling of a word at any time; first, as quickly as possible, but secondly in a way that would make it possible for them to imprint the word they were looking for permanently on their minds. It is chiefly when writing and correcting compositions that the spelling of words becomes an interesting and urgent question for the pupil. Frequently asking the teacher or fellow-pupils disturbs the latter in their work, also encourages a certain intellectual laziness, and the information supplied by a fellow-pupil is moreover often incorrect. In addition, however, an oral communication leaves a much weaker impression on the memory than the word one has seen. Only the dictionary makes it possible to hold the pupil fully responsible for the spelling in his work, for it gives him a reliable means of finding and correcting his mistakes, if he so wishes. But it is absolutely necessary that the pupil should correct his composition independently. He should feel himself to be the sole author of his work and also solely responsible for it. Only independent correction also enables the teacher to form a correct picture of the pupil’s knowledge and intelligence. Exchanging exercise books and correcting one another’s work produces, so to speak, a blurred picture of the abilities of the class. From the work of pupil A I do not want at the same time to learn what pupil B can do; I want to see that from B’s work. Nor does mutual correction, as is sometimes claimed, even provide a correct picture of the general level of the class (for that, every pupil would have to correct the work of all his fellow-pupils, which is of course impossible). I also believe that such average spelling is not what should interest the teacher; for it is not “the class” that is supposed to learn to spell correctly, but _each_ pupil: so the pupils had to be given a dictionary; for a word notebook, too, as is often recommended, does not fulfil our purpose. In a word notebook several pages are left blank for each initial letter, and in the available space the pupils occasionally enter important words, in the order in which they arise in instruction. Such a notebook may be good for some purposes, but it is no use for looking things up, as a substitute for the dictionary, since it either contains far too few words, or looking them up takes an enormous amount of time and is therefore practically impossible. So: a word*book*, but which one? Only the two dictionaries of the Schoolbook Publishing House come into consideration. The large edition of the same, which for the sake of brevity I shall call “the large dictionary” below, has various disadvantages for my purpose. First, it is too extensive and consequently in many cases too expensive for our rural population; secondly, because of its size it is difficult for children to use; thirdly, it contains a great many words which the child never uses—especially many foreign words—while it does not contain many words which would be necessary for children. Some of these are words which perhaps were not included because of their simplicity, e.g. “dann”, “wann”, “mir”, “dir”, “du”, “in”, and others. Yet it is precisely these simplest words that children very often spell incorrectly and that provide the occasion for the most regrettable mistakes. On the other hand, we miss many compounds and derivatives in the large dictionary which belong in a dictionary for elementary schools because children find it difficult to recognize them as such and therefore often do not think of looking up the root words (e.g. “Rauchfang”—the children say “Raufang”). Or they recognize the word as a compound, but are mistaken in analysing it and look up “ein” and “Name”, for example, for the word “Einnahme”, etc. etc. For the reasons given, the large dictionary was unsuitable for my purpose; but the small edition is completely useless: for it lacks most of the simple and important words of everyday life; indeed, this little book is almost only a dictionary of foreign words, precisely what I could not use. In this predicament I decided to dictate a dictionary to my pupils (of the fourth class of a five-class school). This dictionary comprised approximately 2,500 headwords. A dictionary of still smaller scope would not have fulfilled its purpose. Anyone engaged in practice can form an idea of the difficulty of this work, which is intended to result in every pupil receiving a clean and, if possible, error-free dictionary, for to achieve that the teacher must check almost every word with every pupil. (Spot checks are not enough. I shall say nothing about the demands this makes on discipline.) When this little dictionary was finished after several months’ work, it became clear that the work had been worth the effort, for the improvement in spelling was astonishing. The orthographical conscience had been awakened. But this procedure of compiling a dictionary oneself is not generally practicable, especially not in schools of lower organization, and even in more highly organized schools it is so time-consuming and difficult that these disadvantages more than outweigh the advantages which a self-compiled dictionary undoubtedly has over one bought ready-made. Thus I came to write the present dictionary.

The problems arising in compiling the dictionary concern the selection and arrangement of the words. The following considerations guided me in selecting the words:

1.) The dictionary is to include only, but all, those words with which Austrian elementary-school pupils are familiar. Thus it is not to include many good German words which are uncommon in Austria; e.g. “abgefeimt”, “aeffen”, “bosseln”, “erklecklich”, etc. etc. Space must be economized, since great length makes looking things up more difficult and makes the book more expensive. On the other hand, within the range of words familiar to the pupil, the greatest possible completeness is necessary, if only because frequent unsuccessful attempts to look something up make the pupil uncertain and lead him to stop consulting the dictionary. 2.) No word is too simple to be included—for I have experienced “wo” being written with a lengthening h and “was” with ss.

3.) Compounds are to be included where they are either difficult for the child to recognize as such or where looking up the root words easily leads to mistakes.

4.) Foreign words are to be included if they are in general use. Their German equivalent is to be given where it is not too cumbersome or less comprehensible than the foreign word.

5.) Expressions from dialect are to be included only insofar as they have entered the educated language, such as, for example, “Heferl”, “Packel”, “Lacke”, and others.

In individual cases it is of course difficult to decide whether or not a word should be included. Far more difficult, however, are the questions raised by the arrangement of the words. For besides the principle of alphabetical order, various principles which cross one another determine the arrangement, and which is to be the determining principle in a given case depends not infrequently on the author’s subjective view. One such principle is to attach derived words to the root word (i.e. only the root word is a headword; the other words follow it on the same line or on the lines following it; in the latter case, the following lines are indented). This principle intersects with the principle of alphabetical order. How, for example, are the words “alt”, “Altar”, “Alter”, “Altertum”, “altertuemlich” to be arranged? Here we have alphabetical order; its disadvantage is that “alt” and “Alter”, which clearly belong together, are separated by a word of heterogeneous meaning. But bringing related words together is desirable already for reasons of saving space. Now this principle would require “Altertum” and “altertümlich” also to be attached to the word “alt”, and thus to be moved before the word “Altar”; but this arrangement would again appear unnatural and would make finding the complicated derivatives much more difficult. In this case I arranged the words as follows:

_**alt**, das Alter_

_der **Altar**_

_Das **Altertum**, altertümlich_

_etc._

I have given this example here because it shows how the arrangement of the words is governed by various points of view, whose claims are often difficult to weigh against one another. Someone might perhaps recommend the principle of alphabetical order as the sole governing principle (this is how it is carried out, for example, in the Weide dictionary). But purely alphabetical order, where it inserts heterogeneous words between closely related ones, makes demands on the child’s powers of abstraction that are too high, in my opinion, and is often not to be recommended for reasons of understanding the words and of the—extremely important—saving of space. And equally, any adherence to a rigid principle leads to arrangements unsuited to our purpose and must be abandoned—however much this might facilitate the author’s work. Rather, it is necessary to make compromises again and again. In one case, attaching the derivative to the root word easily leads to confusion; in another, this danger is absent; in some cases the root word is quite uncommon, while the derivative is in common use, and it is advisable to place the latter before the former; here a compound is to be placed beside the headword because it makes its meaning clear and guards against misunderstandings, there this is superfluous; etc. etc. It would take me too far to justify my arrangement in a larger number of cases. I considered it carefully and at length in each individual case. Again and again psychological principles (where will the pupil look for the word, how will he best be protected from confusion, etc.) intersect with grammatical ones (root word, derivative), and with typographical ones (use of space, surveyability of the appearance of the text, etc.). Thus it comes about that to the superficial judge apparently arbitrary inconsistencies confront him at every turn, though they are conditioned by compromises between the relevant points of view.

I have used bold type, apart from emphasizing the headwords, wherever I wanted to make words or individual letters particularly conspicuous. The reasons should not be difficult to understand in each particular case. Here too, however, it was not appropriate to decide according to _one_ principle alone whether a word or letter should be printed in bold or not (for example, to print all root words in bold, but not the derivatives).

Where it was decisive for the alphabetical order of the words, I treated ss as a simple s-sound. The customary arrangement, which places it after ss, seemed to me unnatural in a large number of cases and liable to make it more difficult for children to find a word; for example, when the words “ausgiebig”, “Auskunft”, “Ausnahme”, etc. are inserted between “aus” and “aussen”. The pupil reads “aus”, finds nothing at “ause” and thinks, “ah, then I already know how “ausen” is spelt anyway.” Of course my arrangement too produces some unnatural features, because in present-day spelling -sss- is used as sz and as -ß- and would therefore belong in a different place in the alphabet in each of these two cases.

One more word about placing the article before the headword: I believe that this facilitates understanding and prevents some mistakes. I have added the article to all nouns (with the exception of some compounds), since it emphasizes the noun as such. But the postposed article is easily overlooked by the child, or incorrectly taken to refer to the word following it. The surveyability of the column should not have suffered from the new arrangement.

Otterthal 22.4.1925.

_The Author._
