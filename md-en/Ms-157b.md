For how can logic lose its strictness?! Of course, not by discussing something about it, but only through a regrouping in which the idea of strictness is given a different place. For example, by recognizing the ideal of order as part of the mode of representation. By ceasing to frantically search the world for what is drawn on our glasses. Then, however, the glasses must be taken off and exchanged for other glasses. A pair of glasses, without which seeing is unthinkable, is not a pair of glasses. The simile of the glasses is then wrongly applied and misleading.

“An ‘indefinite sense’ would actually be _no_ sense at all”: this is like saying: “an unclear boundary is actually no boundary at all”. One might think something like this: if I say: “I have locked this man firmly in the room (only one door is open)” – then I have not actually locked him in; he is only locked in _in appearance_. Here one forgets that the locked doors _do_ actually achieve something. One would be inclined to say here: so you haven’t _done anything_ with that. And yet he has done something. A boundary that has a hole in it – one might say – is as good as _no_ boundary at all. But is that true?

Imagine a material harder & more solid than any other. But if one brings a rod made of this material from the horizontal into the vertical position, it contracts, or imagine it bends when it is upright, while it is so hard that it cannot be bent in any other way. A mechanism made of this material, such as a crankshaft, connecting rod & crosshead. Different movement of the crosshead.

![](../graphics/157b,19v.svg)

Or: a rod bends when a certain mass is brought near it, but against all the forces that we let act on it, it is completely rigid. Imagine the guide rails bend & straighten again when the crankshaft approaches them & moves away again. But I assume that no special external force is needed to bring this about. This behaviour of the rails would seem like that of a living being. If we say “if the members of the mechanism were completely rigid, they would move in this way,” what is the criterion for them being completely rigid? Is it that they resist certain forces? Or that they move in a certain way? Suppose I say: “this is the law of motion of the crosshead (the correlation of its position with the position of the crankshaft, for example, when the length of the crankshaft & the connecting rod does not change.” This probably means that if the positions of the crankshaft & the crosshead are related in this way, then I say that the length of the connecting rod remains the same. “If the parts were completely rigid, they would move in this way”: is this a hypothesis? It seems not. For if we say: “kinematics describes the movements of the mechanism under the assumption that its parts are completely rigid,” then we admit, on the one hand, that this assumption never applies in reality, and on the other hand, it should be beyond all doubt that completely rigid parts would move in this way. But where does this certainty come from? It is probably not a matter of certainty, but of a determination that we have made. We do not _know_ that bodies, if they were rigid (according to the & the criteria), would move in this way; rather, we would (under certain circumstances) call parts “rigid” that move in this way.

![](../graphics/157b,21v.svg)

In such a case, always remember that geometry (or kinematics) does not specify a method of measurement when it speaks of equal length or of the constancy of a length. If we call kinematics, for example, the doctrine of the movement of completely rigid machine parts, then this contains, on the one hand, an indication of the (mathematical) method: we determine certain distances as the lengths of machine parts that do not change; on the other hand, an _indication_ of the application of the calculus.
