# Ms-141

### <span class="fac">[1\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-141/1.webp)</span>

Augustine's description of learning language. This view is the natural one for a large part of humanity. One thinks of how the child learns the names of people & words like “man”, “chair”, “sugar”. One does not think here of components of language like “now”, “not”, “but”, “all”. Augustine's view of learning language could be called incomplete, deficient. As if someone looked at a forest of deciduous trees, conifers, bushes, ferns & other things, & said that the forest consisted of pines. But it is important that we can conceive of a language (system of communication) for which Augustine's description holds.

### <span class="fac">[1\[2\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-141/1.webp)</span>

<span class="series-number">1)</span> Let us imagine a language whose function is that a builder A communicates through it with a labourer B. B is to pass building-stones to A. There are cuboids, columns, slabs, beams. The language consists of the words: “cuboid”, “column”, “slab”, “beam”; A calls one of these words to B, whereupon B brings a building-stone of a particular shape. The child learns this language from adults; it is trained in the use of the language. In doing so, a building-stone is pointed out, the child's attention is directed to it, & a word is spoken. This may be called “ostensive word-teaching”. In the actual use of this language one party calls out the words, as commands, the other acts on them; but learning the language may include the process whereby the learner merely ‘names’ things, i.e. says the words of the language when the things are pointed at. Indeed, this learning also includes the simpler exercise: the learner repeats the words spoken to him by the adult.

### <span class="fac">[1\[3\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-141/1.webp)</span>

<span class="series-number">2)</span> Let us consider an extension of this language: The labourer knows the series of words “one” to “ten” by heart; on the command “five slabs!” he goes to the place where the slabs lie, says the series from “one” to “five” & picks up a slab at each word; then he brings the five to the builder. (Here both parties use the language in speech.) In learning this language, memorizing the number-words will occupy an important place. – The application of the individual number-words will again be taught ostensively. But now the same word (say “three”) is taught both by pointing to slabs and by pointing to cuboids etc., & on the other hand different number-words by pointing to different groups of stones of the same kind.

### <span class="fac">[1\[4\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-141/1.webp)</span>

<span class="series-number">3)</span> Let us introduce further devices of the language: A particular object (building-stone) is given – by pointing – a name (proper name); when the name is called out, B brings the object. Ostensive teaching is different here again; compare it with the earlier case.

### <span class="fac">[2\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-141/2.webp)</span>

<span class="series-number">4)</span> On the command “The slab there!” B brings the slab that is pointed at; on the command “Slab there!” he brings a slab to the place that is pointed to. Is the use of the words “there” & “there-to” also taught ostensively? N.B.: one does not give a place the name “there”.

### <span class="fac">[2\[2\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-141/2.webp)</span>

<span class="series-number">5)</span> Question & answer. A asks “How many slabs?” – B counts them & answers with the number. Each of these forms of linguistic intercourse could be called a “language-game”. They are more or less related to what we ordinarily call “games”. The child learns the language by means of such games. (And they often then also have the entertaining character of a game.) We do not want to regard language-games as unfinished parts, fragments of a whole, the language, but as languages complete in themselves, systems of human communication. And for this it is useful to imagine that a primitive tribe possesses such a language which, for example, consists only of a system of commands for use in war. – Such a primitive language is immediately related to a primitive arithmetic.

The person who learns the notation of chemistry, descriptive geometry, reading a weather map, etc., also learns new forms of language, language-games.

### <span class="fac">[2\[3\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-141/2.webp)</span>

<span class="series-number">6)</span> A further language-game: Asking for the name. New forms of building-stones are introduced. B points to one & asks: “What is that?” – A answers “That is a …”. If A later calls out the newly introduced word (say “plinth”), B brings the stone. The words “that is …” with the ostensive gesture are called “ostensive explanation”. In this case a generic name (in particular a form-name) was explained. Similarly, one can _ask_ ostensively for the proper name of a particular thing, & likewise for the name of a colour, a number, a direction, etc.

### <span class="fac">[2\[4\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-141/2.webp)</span>

<span class="series-number">7)</span> In a table, written signs are set against pictures. B is provided with the table; A writes one of the signs; B looks for it in the table, looks at or points to the corresponding picture & brings the object that it represents. In each of these language-games we have introduced new kinds of signs. – I want above all to distinguish between sentences & words. I call every complete sign of a language-game a “sentence” here. Its component signs are words. A sentence can also consist of merely one word. Thus in language-game (I) the words “cuboid”, “slab”, etc. are the sentences. In the second language-game a sentence consists of two words. Depending on the role that falls to them in the language-game, we can distinguish commands, questions, descriptions & other kinds among the sentences.

### <span class="fac">[2\[5\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-141/2.webp),[3\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-141/3.webp)</span>

<span class="series-number">8)</span> If, in a language-game resembling No. 1, the call is “Cuboid, slab, column!”, whereupon the assistant passes a cuboid, a slab & a column, one can speak here of three commands (sentences), but also of one (compound command).

### <span class="fac">[3\[2\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-141/3.webp),[4\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-141/4.webp)</span>

But if

<span class="series-number">9)</span> the order of passing is indicated by the order of the words (“cuboid, then slab, then column”), we shall say that A calls out a sentence consisting of three words. Among the words we see groups of those with similar functions. We see an analogy in the use of the words “one”, “two”, “three”, etc. & on the other hand one in the use of “cuboid”, “column”, etc. We therefore distinguish types of word; e.g. number-words & form-words. The sentences in (8) consist of several words of the same type. The function of a word can, in another language-game, pass over to the _order_ of the other words in the sentence. What a word does here is done in another system without such a word. The commands in language-game (9) could have been given with the help of order-words; thus, for example: “Secondly slab, firstly cuboid, thirdly column!”; & these order-words can be eliminated through the order of the form-words. Such a consideration shows how much less rigid the function of the word in the sentence is than logicians often assume. If we group words according to the similarity of their function, they will allow themselves to be grouped differently according to different points of view. Thus, for obvious reasons, one might not want to group the word “one” together with “two”, “three”, etc. (One often says “two or more”.) “White is not a colour”. Words can be compared with chess pieces in many respects. – Think of the different possibilities for separating chess pieces into groups according to their functions in the game. It is natural to count as language, as expression, also the pointing gesture which, for example, accompanies the words in (4). But the pictures in (7) are also means of language. (Augustine.) We call such pictures & other similar means of language “samples”. (This is a vague determination; & is meant to be.) Sample & word have different functions. Something is compared with the sample, not with the word. (But if the word is used onomatopoeically, one could also call it a sample.) But with this distinction we have not established a final duality in logic; we have only singled out two characteristic kinds from the means of our language. One might call “1”, “2”, “3”, “4”, etc. _words_; “∣”, “∣∣”, “∣∣∣”, “∣∣∣∣”, etc., on the other hand, _samples_. Now if, in a language, the number-words were “one”, “one one”, “one one one”, etc., should we call “one” a word or a sample? The same element of language can function here as a word and there as a sample. A circular figure can be the name (word) of an ellipse, but also the sample with which it is to be compared in a language-game according to a particular method of projection.

We call a great variety of processes more or less related to one another “comparison of an object with a sample” & we group language-elements with all kinds of more or less related functions under the word “sample”. In (7) B compares a picture on a table with objects that he has before him. But how does he compare them? – Let the table contain in one case α) pictures of a hammer, a pair of pincers, a chisel & a saw, in another β) twenty pictures of different butterflies of the hawk-moth family. In the second case, comparing the picture with the objects will be quite a different process from that in the first. Again it will be different in case γ), when the pictures are scale drawings of cuboids of different proportions & the comparison takes place, say, with callipers. Etc., etc. The task might be to bring a material of the colour of the sample. But how are the colours of sample & material to be compared?

### <span class="fac">[4\[2\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-141/4.webp)</span>

<span class="series-number">10)</span> Something like this: One shows B the sample, whereupon he fetches the material (“from memory”).

### <span class="fac">[4\[3\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-141/4.webp)</span>

<span class="series-number">11)</span> Or: B is given the sample & looks from the sample at the materials from which he has to choose.

### <span class="fac">[4\[4\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-141/4.webp)</span>

<span class="series-number">12)</span> Or: B holds the sample against the materials in succession & chooses the one that he cannot distinguish from the sample; for which, say, the transition from sample to material disappears.

### <span class="fac">[4\[5\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-141/4.webp),[5\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-141/5.webp),[6\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-141/6.webp)</span>

<span class="series-number">13)</span> On the other hand, let us think of the comparison of colours when the instruction was: “Bring a material somewhat darker than this sample”. In (10) I said that B fetched the material from memory; I thereby made use of a customary way of speaking. But various things could have happened. α) A memory-image of the sample seen floats before B's mind. In choosing the material, he lets the material and the memory-image act on him alternately, by looking at the material, then closing his eyes & imagining the sample. β) No image of the sample floats before B's mind. He looks at the materials in succession, shakes his head each time, feels a certain tension, dissatisfaction; finally he comes to one at whose sight the tension is released; he nods his head & pulls the material out of the heap. γ) The processes are the same as in (β), but without the movements of the head & without the play of sensations. “But why did he then bring this material? How did he recognize it? By what?” – When you ask “why”, are you asking for the cause, or for the reason? If for the cause, – it is easy to think up a physiological or psychological hypothesis according to which the choice under these and these conditions had to turn out as it did. For the rest, it is the business of experimental science to test such hypotheses. If you ask for the reason, the answer is: the choice need not have had a reason. A reason would be a step preceding the step of the choice, which brought the chooser to take this one. But why should one step always be preceded by another? “But then B did not _recognize_ the material he brought as the right one.” – But why should we not also count processes γ as recognition? – “But he now lacks the criterion for recognition! In case α he had, for example, the memory-image, & he recognized the material he was looking for by its agreement with this.” – But did he also have an image of this agreement with him, with which he could compare the agreement, in order to see whether it was the required agreement? And could there not, on the other hand, be such a sample or image of agreement? Does one not explain the word “agreement” to a person by means of examples, & could I not give someone looking for a red object with a red sample, in addition to this, two objects of the same colour, so that he would, so to speak, be reminded by them of what “of the same colour” means? Is any such explanation essentially the last? – And when you say that in case β B recognized what he was looking for through the lessening of the tension, I ask what he recognized the lessening of the tension as. “But in case γ B acts just like an automaton who does not know what he is doing.” – In case γ B has a simpler experience than in α & β. And you probably meant to say that B does not know _why_ he acts in this way. But as far as the cause of the action is concerned, all three cases are on the same level; & one cannot say of the reason that B does not know it; rather, there is no reason. “But what if he brings the material and it does not agree with the sample!” – But that could also have happened in case α. One might then perhaps have said that his memory-image had changed. “But there is surely an essential difference between cases α and γ.” Certainly! namely, the one indicated by their descriptions. In (1) B had learnt to bring a stone of a particular shape on the call “cuboid”. We can imagine this happening as follows: The word awakened in B the image of a cuboid, and the training had, as one would say, established this association. B now picks up a building-stone corresponding to this image, etc. – But did the process have to be this? – If the training could bring it about that the image arose – automatically – on the call, why not also that B reached for the stone of this shape on hearing the word? That means only a small variation of the associative mechanism. The image that arises in this way in response to the word is not formed according to a rule; rather, it is comparable to a little tablet that springs out when a button is pressed. Indeed, we could imagine this aid actually being used instead of the aid of association. The language-device of the image of a colour, a figure, a spatial thing, a sound, etc. is entirely to be placed alongside that of an actually seen colour, figure, etc., etc.

### <span class="fac">[6\[2\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-141/6.webp)</span>

<span class="series-number">14)</span> Training can aim not only at teaching the use of individual tables, but at bringing it about that the learner makes & uses tables with new assignments of written signs & pictures. Language-game: “Make another table!” Or: “Give the things names!”

In training for the use of a table, the exercise may occur of pointing with the finger straight from right to left, as it were drawing parallel horizontal lines.

I shall call tables as well as ostensive explanations & other analogous aids, in agreement with general linguistic usage, “rules”. One is trained in the use of this aid.

### <span class="fac">[6\[3\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-141/6.webp)</span>

The use of a rule can also be explained by a further rule. Example: the use of tables is taught,

These schemata can now be supplied as rules for reading a table. Could one not also give rules for the use of _these_ rules? Certainly. – Is it an incompleteness of the explanation if the use of a rule is not determined by stating a rule? No. The rules have a beginning.

### <span class="fac">[6\[4\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-141/6.webp),[7\[1\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-141/7.webp)</span>

<span class="series-number">16)</span> The decimal notation of cardinal numbers, or a similar series of signs (series of words), is introduced. The learner is encouraged to continue the series independently by analogy. Command: go one step further in the series; continue counting until “Stop” is called; write down _other_ number-words; count things, strokes of a bell, etc.; arrange things in groups of so and so many. Elementary arithmetic, as it is actually taught in primary schools, consists entirely of such language-games. In (7) B brings objects according to commands by means of a table. It may now be that the process is altered, perhaps after some repetitions, so that B brings the object on command without using the table (that is, as in (1)). In such a case one might say that he has now memorized the meanings of the words. – But here again there are various possibilities, similar to those in (10). Instead of the actual table, an imagined one may, for example, be used before this aid disappears altogether. The table, the rule, has now dropped out of the game altogether. It stands in relation to it only as an aid to teaching, training, whereas in (7) it is an instrument of the game. We can therefore distinguish the case in which the game is played _with the help_ of the rule from the case in which it is played in _agreement_ with the rule but without its help.

### <span class="fac">[7\[2\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-141/7.webp)</span>

Chess, in this sense, is ordinarily not played _with the help_ of the rules of chess. But we could imagine a similar game

<span class="series-number">17)</span> in which the players are provided with tables; in one column there are pictures of the various pieces used in the game, in the other schemata assigned to these pictures, which represent the freedom (the permitted moves) of the pieces. Reading the rules for each move is part of the game. The game is played with the help of these rules. If the learner was also given rules for the use of _these_, then those rules (‘rules of the second degree’) do _not_ here belong to the instruments of the game.

### <span class="fac">[7\[3\]](https://cdn.wittgensteinnachlass.com/2000px/webp/Ms-141/7.webp)</span>

15˙1) Language-game: Moving according to signs. Let the sign be “– – x”; one follows it by walking a particular distance, hopping once after every two steps. Similarly one follows the signs “– x x”, “– – x x” & others. The training aims at bringing it about that new signs of this kind, signs whose following the training had not specifically taught, are followed ad hoc. Here we shall certainly say that a sign – x x –, is a rule that shows us how we are to move.

<span class="series-number">15˙2)</span> It would be different if our game consisted only in reacting to the one sign “– – x” in the manner described. Or also if

<span class="series-number">15˙3)</span> the game extended to the use of, say, about 5 such signs. The concept of following, of a rule, is taken from the case of unlimited application. But various things may induce us to consider cases of limited application such as 15˙3, indeed even 15˙2, from the point of view of their similarity to those of unlimited application & to call the signs rules there too. Let us imagine the signs in game 15˙1 formed by dice bearing strokes or crosses (quasi-letters) being assembled into the sign.
